The Warm Homes Loan Scheme lets UK homeowners borrow up to £15,000 for solar panels and another £15,000 for a home battery, at no or reduced interest, repaid over many years. It launched on 27 October 2026 with ten lenders and covers England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. It is a loan, not a grant, so the real question is whether your savings cover the repayments.

## Key numbers

- **£15,000**: maximum loan for solar panels, and **£15,000** for an electrical storage battery (DESNZ factsheet, 27 Oct 2026).
- **Up to 5 percentage points** lower interest than regular loans, with some lenders interest-free (factsheet).
- **10 lenders** at launch, with **up to 30 years** to repay in some cases (factsheet).
- **£2 billion** of government backing for consumer loans within the £15 billion Warm Homes Plan to 2030 (Warm Homes Plan).
- **0% VAT** on installed solar and batteries until **31 March 2027**, then 5% (HMRC Notice 708/6, checked 22 Nov 2026).

## What is the Warm Homes Loan?

It is the consumer finance part of the government's Warm Homes Plan. The plan sets aside £2 billion for consumer loans and describes "low-cost finance to cover the upfront cost of a solar or battery installation" as the main offer for homeowners. Private lenders provide the loans, and government support lowers the interest rate.

The factsheet says the loans let people "install clean energy technology without any upfront cost, instead spreading payments over many years."

## How much can you borrow for solar and a battery?

The factsheet sets a maximum loan per technology:

| Technology | Maximum loan |
|---|---|
| Solar panels | £15,000 |
| Electrical storage battery | £15,000 |
| Air-to-water heat pump | £20,000 |
| Ground source heat pump | £35,000 |
| Micro-renewable system | £20,000 |
| Heat network connection | £7,500 |

For context, the median installed cost of a 4–10 kW MCS solar system in 2025/26 was £1,546 per kW, battery excluded (DESNZ cost data). A 4 kW system at that median is about £6,184, well within the £15,000 solar limit. See [how much solar panels cost in the UK](/uk/guides/how-much-do-solar-panels-cost-uk) for the full breakdown.

## Which lenders offer the Warm Homes Loan?

The factsheet lists ten lenders at launch:

- HomeServe (partnering with E.ON)
- Hometree Finance
- Propensio
- Secure Trust Bank
- Selina Finance
- South Manchester Credit Union
- Sunsave
- Tandem Bank
- The Money Co-op
- thisbank

It says further lenders and products are expected to join. Some lenders work only with their own installer partners, while others let you pick any MCS-certified installer. South Manchester Credit Union, for example, says it will serve residents of Central and South Manchester.

## What interest rate will I pay?

The government has not set a single rate. The factsheet says it has worked with lenders to deliver "an up-to-5 per cent reduction in interest rates from regular loans", and that some lenders offer interest-free loans.

The factsheet gives its own example: a homeowner borrowing £10,000 at 8% over seven years would repay around £155 a month on a normal loan, and could save up to £285 a year on repayments through the scheme. Ask each lender for the APR, the total amount repayable, any fees and whether the rate is fixed.

## Who is eligible?

Households across the whole UK. The factsheet does not set an income limit. The lender carries out "normal affordability, credit and eligibility checks". The installation must be done by an MCS-certified installer (heat network connections are the only exception).

If your income is lower, check grants first. In England, the [Warm Homes: Local Grant](https://www.gov.uk/apply-warm-homes-local-grant) can pay for improvements that may include solar panels, for privately owned homes with an EPC of D to G and household income usually of £36,000 or less. In Scotland, see [what Home Energy Scotland funds now](/uk/guides/home-energy-scotland-solar). For the scheme that is closing, see [ECO4 ends on 31 December 2026](/uk/blog/eco4-ending-december-2026).

## How do I apply?

The factsheet sets out these steps:

1. Consider which upgrades suit your home.
2. Check whether you are eligible for a grant.
3. Compare installation quotes and loans. Look at the total repayable, the term and monthly fees.
4. Apply through a participating lender or installer.
5. Keep records: quote, finance agreement, invoice, proof of purchase and MCS certificate.
6. Check whether a smart or time-of-use tariff could increase your savings.

## Does a loan make solar worth it?

A loan does not change how much electricity your roof produces, so the basic sums stay the same. What changes is timing: you pay over years instead of up front. Compare the monthly repayment with the monthly saving you can realistically expect, and remember that savings are much lower in winter (see [do solar panels work in winter in the UK?](/uk/guides/solar-panels-winter-uk)).

The government says a heat pump, battery and solar panels together could save "up to £550 a year", and a further £300 with a time-of-use tariff. These are government estimates for a full package, not a promise for a solar-only install.

Two other points matter:

- **VAT.** Installed solar and batteries are zero-rated for VAT until 31 March 2027, then 5%. A loan can make finishing before the deadline more realistic. See [Solar panels VAT: 0% until 31 March 2027](/uk/guides/solar-panels-vat-deadline-2027).
- **Export income.** What you earn for exported power depends on your tariff. Compare [Smart Export Guarantee rates](/uk/guides/best-seg-rates) before you size the system.

For a battery, read [when home batteries pay with time-of-use tariffs](/uk/guides/solar-battery-worth-it-uk) before you borrow the extra £15,000.

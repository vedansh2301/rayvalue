For many California homeowners solar still pays in 2026, because electricity here costs about 1.6 times the national average (29.75 vs 18.16 cents per kWh in August 2026, per EIA). But the way it pays has changed. Under NEM 3.0, a kWh you send to the grid at midday is often worth a few cents, while a kWh you use yourself avoids a price near 30 cents. With no federal tax credit and a new fixed charge, solar works best for homes that size it to their own use, often with a battery, and buy at a competitive price.

## Key numbers

- **29.75 cents per kWh:** California average residential price, August 2026, vs 18.16 cents for the U.S. (EIA, preliminary).
- **503 kWh a month:** average California household use in 2024, vs 863 kWh nationally (EIA Table 5A).
- **About 0.4 cents to $1.13 per kWh:** range of PG&E's 2026 weekday export values from an April 1 p.m. hour to an August 6 p.m. hour (PG&E price sheet).
- **About $24 a month:** PG&E's Base Services Charge for most customers from March 2026, which solar cannot reduce (PG&E).
- **$0:** the federal 25D credit for systems placed in service after December 31, 2025 (IRS).
- **$2.4 to $6.3 per watt:** range of median prices across the top-100 U.S. residential installers in 2025, before incentives (Berkeley Lab).

## Why solar can still pay in California

The main driver is the price of grid power. EIA's August 2026 figure for California was 29.75 cents per kWh, down slightly from 30.92 cents a year earlier, and well above the U.S. average of 18.16 cents. Every kWh of solar you use at home avoids that price.

California homes also use less electricity than average, 503 kWh a month in 2024 per EIA. That means smaller systems, which can keep the upfront cost down.

The CPUC's December 2022 fact sheet estimated that new solar customers would pay off their systems in "nine years or less." That estimate came with the statement that the decision "supplements and bolsters federal incentives provided by the Inflation Reduction Act." For 2026 installs the federal residential credit is gone, so do not use that figure as a benchmark.

## Who solar is most likely to pay for in California

- **Homes with high evening and summer use**, especially with air conditioning, an EV or a heat pump.
- **Owners who size to their own use**, not to maximize exports.
- **Homes adding a battery** to shift solar into peak hours. Berkeley Lab reports California drove much of the jump in U.S. residential storage attachment, from 25% of installs in 2024 to 37% in 2025.
- **Buyers who compare quotes.** Median prices across the top-100 installers ranged from $2.4 to $6.3 per watt in 2025, and roughly 60% were below $4 per watt.
- **Homeowners who can finish before January 1, 2027**, if their county assessor confirms the system qualifies for the property-tax exclusion.

## Who it may not pay for

- **Low-use homes** where the fixed charge is a large share of the bill.
- **Shaded roofs** or roofs that need replacing soon.
- **Anyone relying on a quote that includes the federal credit** for a 2026 install. See [red flags in a solar quote](/blog/solar-quote-red-flags-2026).
- **NEM 2 owners thinking of expanding.** Adding more than the greater of 10% or 1 kW can move you off NEM 2. See [adding a battery to NEM 2](/guides/add-battery-to-nem-2-system).

## What does solar plus a battery cost?

The DOE's 2025 Q1 benchmark, in 2024 dollars, puts an 8 kW system with a 13.5 kWh battery at $4.33 to $4.59 per watt, against $2.78 to $2.95 per watt for solar alone. That is a national modeled benchmark, not a California quote. Berkeley Lab's 2025 data, mostly from California, put the median premium for paired solar plus storage at $2.1 per watt.

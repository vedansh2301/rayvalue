In Florida, net metering lets your solar offset the electricity you buy, kWh for kWh, during the year. If your utility is investor-owned (FPL, Duke Energy Florida, Tampa Electric), extra solar carries forward as kWh credits. Credits you haven't used by the end of the year are paid out at a much lower wholesale-type rate. Size your system to your yearly use, not beyond it, and remember that fixed charges stay on your bill.

## Key numbers

- **Credit:** excess kWh in one month are credited to the next month's usage, and credits roll forward for **up to 12 months** (Rule 25-6.065(8)(e)-(f), last amended **April 7, 2008**).
- **Year-end payout:** unused credits are paid at an average annual rate based on the utility's **COG-1 as-available energy tariff**. FPL calls this its "average annual cost of generation" and credits it on the **December** bill (FPL FAQ, read November 2026).
- **Tier 1:** **10 kW AC or less**. AC rating = DC panel rating × **0.85**, so roughly **11.7 kW DC**.
- **Tier 1 fees:** no extra application fees beyond what other customers pay (Rule 25-6.065(4)(e)).
- **Minimum bills:** FPL has a **$30** minimum base bill (October 2026 rate sheet). Tampa Electric's RS minimum charge is the basic service charge of **$0.45 per day** (Tariff Section 6, read November 2026).
- **Federal credit:** the 25D residential credit is **$0** for systems installed in 2026 or later (IRS).

## What is net metering in Florida?

Florida law defines net metering as "a metering and billing methodology whereby customer-owned renewable generation is allowed to offset the customer's electricity consumption on site" ([s. 366.91](https://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0300-0399/0366/Sections/0366.91.html)). The same statute told public utilities to set up a standard interconnection agreement and net metering program, and told municipal utilities and rural electric cooperatives to develop their own.

For investor-owned utilities, the Florida Public Service Commission wrote the details into [Rule 25-6.065](https://www.flrules.org/gateway/ruleNo.asp?id=25-6.065). The rule says it "applies to all investor-owned utilities". If you buy power from a city utility or a co-op, your terms come from that utility, so ask it for its net metering policy.

## How FPL net metering works (and Duke and TECO)

Under the rule, the utility must:

1. Install a meter that measures power in both directions "at no additional cost to the customer".
2. Read it monthly.
3. Bill you normally for any power you use beyond what your panels supply.
4. Credit any excess you send to the grid against next month's usage.
5. Let those credits build up and offset usage "for a period of not more than twelve months".
6. At the end of each calendar year, pay you for unused credits at an average annual rate based on its COG-1 as-available energy tariff.

FPL's [FAQ](https://www.fpl.com/clean-energy/net-metering/faq.html) describes the same thing in bill terms. Excess solar shows up as a "kWh reserve" on your bill. "You don't receive any monetary credit for the energy your system produces," FPL says. Instead you get kWh credit, and any surplus at the end of the year is credited on your December bill at FPL's average annual cost of generation.

Duke Energy Florida and Tampa Electric run under the same rule. We have not yet confirmed their own billing wording for this guide. See our [Duke Energy Florida](/us/utility/florida/duke-energy-florida) and [TECO](/us/utility/florida/teco) pages for their rates, and [FPL](/us/utility/florida/fpl) for FPL's.

## What happens to extra solar credits in December?

That year-end payout is where oversizing costs you. During the year, each credited kWh replaces a kWh you would have bought at the full retail price. At year end, leftover kWh are paid at the utility's as-available energy rate, which is a generation cost, not a retail price. FPL says its rate is recalculated every year.

So the panels that produce more than you use over a full year earn much less than the rest. The practical rule: size the system to your annual use or a bit under it. [How many solar panels you need](/guides/how-many-solar-panels-do-i-need) walks through that sizing from your bill.

## Net metering tiers: why 10 kW matters

The rule sorts systems into three tiers by AC "gross power rating":

| Tier | Size (AC) | What the rule allows the utility to require |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 kW or less | No extra fees; no further design review if equipment meets listed standards |
| 2 | Over 10 kW up to 100 kW | Liability insurance up to $1 million; a PSC-approved application fee |
| 3 | Over 100 kW up to 2 MW | Liability insurance up to $2 million; fee and possible interconnection study charge |

For inverter systems, AC rating is the DC nameplate rating multiplied by 0.85. A 12 kW DC array is therefore 10.2 kW AC and lands in Tier 2. FPL's FAQ confirms that Tier 2 and Tier 3 systems "require proof of liability insurance and an application fee" and need a visible manual disconnect switch. Most homes don't need anything near that size, but if a quote is just over the line, ask whether a slightly smaller array keeps you in Tier 1.

## Fixed charges and minimum bills still apply

The rule says that whatever you send to the grid, "the customer shall continue to pay the applicable customer charge". In practice:

- **FPL:** a $30 minimum base bill. FPL's rate sheet says residential customers "whose monthly base electric service costs fall below $30 are subject to a minimum $30 base bill". FPL explains it as covering poles, transformers and wires that net metering customers still use.
- **Tampa Electric:** RS has a basic service charge of $0.45 per day, and "MINIMUM CHARGE: The Basic Service Charge."

Anyone promising a "$0 electric bill" from solar in FPL territory is wrong. See [Florida solar scams and red flags](/guides/florida-solar-scams).

## How to sign up

Your installer usually files the paperwork. FPL's process: you or your contractor submit the application and sign the Interconnection Agreement for Customer-Owned Renewable Generation. The rule sets the timing for investor-owned utilities:

- Within **10 business days** of receiving your application, the utility must confirm it has everything or say what's missing.
- It must sign the agreement within **30 calendar days** of a complete application.
- You must sign and return it at least **30 calendar days** before you start running the system in parallel with the grid.

Don't switch the system on before the utility approves it.

## Is net metering a good deal in Florida?

For a system sized to your own yearly use, yes. Credited kWh offset retail-priced kWh, and the grid works like a free 12-month battery. That is a big part of why a home battery adds less bill savings in Florida than in California. (Our [Florida hurricane battery guide](/guides/florida-hurricane-battery-backup) covers the backup case instead.)

The limits: fixed charges stay, oversizing earns little, and you have no federal credit to cut the cost. Run your own numbers in the [Florida solar payback calculator](/us/solar-payback/florida).

## The short answer

For some homes solar still pays back, and for others it does not. Without the federal credit you pay the full installed cost, so payback takes longer than it did when part of the cost came back as a credit. The only way to know is to run your own numbers with your utility's rates.

## What ended, and when

The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, according to the IRS. RayValue applies no federal credit to any 2026 or later install.

## What still exists

Other incentives can exist at the state, local and utility level. We only list incentives we have verified against a primary source, and we have not added any to this guide yet, so check with your installer and your utility, and ask them to show the source. Be wary of any quote that counts on the federal credit for a 2026 install.

## How payback changed

With the credit gone, the upfront cost you need to earn back is higher, so payback is longer for the same system. What you save each year does not change: it depends on your electric rate, how much of your usage the panels cover, and how the utility credits power you send to the grid. That is why the same system can pay back sooner in one utility's territory than another's.

## Who it can still make sense for

- Homes with high electric bills, where each kilowatt-hour solar replaces is worth more.
- Roofs with good sun and little shade.
- Owners who plan to stay long enough to earn the cost back.
- Homeowners who also want backup power from a battery, understanding that a battery is mostly about resilience.

## Who it may not make sense for

- Homes with small bills, where the fixed cost of an installation is hard to earn back.
- Heavily shaded roofs.
- Renters, and owners who expect to move soon.

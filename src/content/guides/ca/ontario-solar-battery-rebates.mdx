Ontario homeowners can get up to $5,000 back on rooftop solar and up to $5,000 more on a battery through the Home Renovation Savings program, so up to $10,000 in total. The money is real and no energy assessment is needed. The trade-off is that rebate systems cannot use net metering, so the best fit is a home that uses its own solar power, ideally with a battery.

## Key numbers

- **$1,000 per kW** of solar, up to 50% of total costs, max **$5,000** (Home Renovation Savings, checked 22 Nov 2026).
- **$300 per kWh** of battery storage, up to 50% of costs, max **$5,000** (same source).
- **No energy assessment** required for these two rebates (same source).
- **No net metering** for rebate recipients (Home Renovation Savings; Toronto Hydro).
- **1,276 kWh per kW per year**: modelled solar output in Toronto (NREL PVWatts v8, run 22 Nov 2026).
- **18.9¢ on-peak, 9.1¢ off-peak**: time-of-use prices from 1 Nov 2026 (Hydro One, quoting OEB).

## What rebates are available for solar in Ontario in 2026?

The main program is Home Renovation Savings, delivered by Save on Energy (the IESO's brand) and Enbridge Gas. It covers two measures on its solar page:

| Measure | Rate | Cap |
|---|---|---|
| Rooftop solar panels | $1,000 per kW | 50% of total costs, up to $5,000 |
| Battery energy storage | $300 per kWh | 50% of costs, up to $5,000 |

You can take the solar rebate on its own or combine it with the battery rebate. The program says a home energy assessment is not required.

## How do the caps work?

Each rebate is the lowest of three numbers: the per-unit amount, half of the cost, and $5,000. Some simple examples:

- **4 kW of solar** costing $14,000: $4,000 (4 x $1,000). Half the cost is $7,000, so the per-kW amount is the limit.
- **7 kW of solar** costing $20,000: $7,000 by the per-kW rate, but the cap is $5,000.
- **10 kWh battery** costing $12,000: $3,000 (10 x $300).
- **16.7 kWh battery or larger**: reaches the $5,000 cap, as long as $5,000 is no more than half the cost.

These costs are illustrations of the formula, not price quotes.

## Who qualifies?

The program lists these conditions. You must:

- be at least 18;
- live in a single detached, semi-detached, row house, townhome or mobile home on a permanent foundation;
- own the home where the system is installed (a landlord with tenants applies as the owner) and pay for the purchase and installation;
- be connected to the Ontario electricity grid. Cornwall Electric customers are on the Hydro-Québec grid and are not eligible.

Condo apartment owners are not on the housing list.

## What are the battery rules?

The battery must be paired with a **new** solar PV system and "sized appropriately" for it. It should be designed to store mainly solar power rather than grid power. That rules out a battery-only install charged from cheap overnight power under this program.

A battery fits the program's design well. Rebate systems are meant for "load displacement", where solar output is used to meet the home's own needs. A battery stores the midday surplus for the evening peak, which is when Ontario's time-of-use price is highest (18.9¢ on-peak versus 9.1¢ off-peak). It can also keep some circuits running in an outage if the system is designed for backup.

## How do I apply?

The program sets out four steps:

1. **Confirm eligibility** on the Home Renovation Savings site.
2. **Choose a contractor and get approval.** Your contractor submits a pre-installation form and helps get connection approval from your local utility.
3. **Install.** Only after written pre-approval from Home Renovation Savings and your utility. The program says not to buy or install equipment before that.
4. **Receive the rebate** once the contractor submits the documents and the application is approved.

The program does not endorse contractors. It points to the CanREA solar guide as one place to find them.

## What do I give up by taking the rebate?

Net metering. The program says participants who receive the solar and storage incentive cannot sign a net metering agreement with their utility. Toronto Hydro says the same on its solar page. Without net metering, surplus solar power sent to the grid earns no bill credit.

That is why the rebate works best when most of your output is used at home. If your home is empty all day and you do not want a battery, read [Ontario solar rebate vs net metering](/ca/guides/ontario-solar-rebate-vs-net-metering) before you decide. It shows a simple way to compare the two.

## Who does the rebate suit best?

- **Solar plus battery buyers.** You get up to $10,000 back and keep the surplus on site.
- **Homes with daytime demand**: people working from home, heat pumps, pool pumps, or an EV charged at midday.
- **Systems around 5 kW**, where the $5,000 solar cap is reached and is a large share of the cost.

## How much power will the panels make?

NREL's PVWatts model gives about 1,276 kWh per kW per year for a south-facing roof at a 20 degree tilt in Toronto. So 5 kW would make about 6,380 kWh a year. Real output depends on shading, roof direction and snow. Each kWh used at home avoids buying it at your rate plan's price.

## How do I avoid rebate scams?

The program warns about fake websites, unauthorized rebate offers and people impersonating its contractors. Apply only through the official Home Renovation Savings site, and never share personal details with someone who contacts you out of the blue.

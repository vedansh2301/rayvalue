A 3 kW rooftop system in India makes roughly **330 to 420 units a month on average**, according to the European Commission's PVGIS model. That assumes the panels face south at a good tilt with no shade. Delhi comes out at about **405** units and Mumbai about **380**. Output swings with the season. March is usually the best month, while monsoon months can fall to **around 210–300** in western and central cities.

## Key numbers

- **3,986 to 5,030 kWh a year** for 3 kW: PVGIS range across 15 major cities, Guwahati to Jodhpur (EU JRC PVGIS 5.3, retrieved 22 Nov 2026).
- **1,329 to 1,677 kWh per kW per year** across all 45 cities we queried.
- **Over 300 units a month**: the government's figure for a 3 kW system (PIB factsheet, 13 Mar 2025).
- **19%**: capacity utilisation factor KERC assumes for home solar in its 2026 tariff order. That works out to about 1,664 kWh per kW per year.
- **14%**: system losses assumed in the PVGIS figures below.

PVGIS uses satellite and reanalysis weather data (ERA5) for 2005–2023, so these figures are long-run averages, not a forecast for any one year. Chennai's weakest month is November because of the north-east monsoon.

## Is the "300 units a month" figure right?

PIB's PM Surya Ghar factsheet says "a 3-kW system can generate over 300 units per month on average". The scheme's "up to 300 units free" message is built on it.

On PVGIS's numbers, it is a **fair, slightly cautious annual average** for every city in the table. But it is not true every month. In Mumbai, Pune, Ahmedabad and Kolkata, July output for a 3 kW system is well under 300 units. If your bill is highest in summer, this works in your favour. If you plan around a steady 300 units, expect monsoon bills to rise.

KERC's own assumption for Karnataka home solar is a **19% capacity utilisation factor**. That works out to 0.19 × 8,760 hours ≈ 1,664 units per kW per year, a little above PVGIS's 1,519 for Bengaluru.

## How many units per day?

Divide the annual figure by 365. A 3 kW system in Delhi averages about 13 units a day, and one in Kolkata about 12. Per kW, Indian cities average roughly **3.6 to 4.6 units a day** across the year.

## What reduces output on a real roof?

PVGIS assumes an ideal setup. Real systems lose output to:

- **Shade** from water tanks, parapets, trees and taller buildings. Even partial shade on one panel can cut a string's output.
- **Orientation and tilt.** Flat-mounted or north-leaning panels produce less. Many Indian roofs use a fixed tilt that is not optimal.
- **Dust.** Panels in dry, dusty areas need regular cleaning. MNRE requires vendors to provide five years of free maintenance.
- **Heat.** Panels lose efficiency in high temperatures. PVGIS includes a temperature model, but rooftop ventilation matters.
- **Inverter sizing and outages.** On-grid systems switch off during power cuts. See [On-grid vs hybrid vs off-grid](/in/guides/on-grid-vs-hybrid-vs-off-grid).

Ask your vendor for a generation estimate, and compare it with the table above. A quote far above PVGIS for your city deserves a hard question.

## How should I size my system?

MNRE's leaflet suggests 1–2 kW for 0–150 units a month, 2–3 kW for 150–300, and above 3 kW for higher use. Using the table:

1. Find your **average monthly units** from a year of bills.
2. Divide by your city's **per-kW monthly average**, which is the 3 kW figure divided by 3. In Delhi that is about 135 units per kW.
3. Round to a sensible size. Don't go far above your use, because surplus is often bought cheaply or, in Tamil Nadu under net metering, lapses on 31 March. See [Net metering by state](/in/guides/net-metering-by-state).

The PM Surya Ghar subsidy stops growing at 3 kW (₹78,000). See [PM Surya Ghar subsidy 2026](/in/guides/pm-surya-ghar-subsidy-2026).

## What are those units worth?

That depends entirely on your tariff slab. In Maharashtra a unit used can save over ₹19 at the top slab. On subsidised low slabs it may save under ₹3.

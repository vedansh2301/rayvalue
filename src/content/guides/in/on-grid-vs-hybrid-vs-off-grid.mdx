**On-grid** solar connects to your DISCOM through a net meter. It is the cheapest option and the one the PM Surya Ghar subsidy was built for, but it gives no power during a grid outage. **Hybrid** solar adds a battery. It can still get the subsidy if your state regulator allows the setup, but the subsidy is paid only on the panels. **Off-grid** solar is not connected to the grid at all, and **gets no PM Surya Ghar subsidy**.

## Key numbers

- **₹78,000**: maximum PM Surya Ghar subsidy (₹85,800 in special category states). It is based only on solar module capacity, whatever battery or inverter you add (MNRE).
- **Off-grid: ₹0** subsidy under the scheme (MNRE guidelines).
- **₹1.96 to ₹3.89 per kWh**: what Karnataka pays for exported solar, depending on subsidy and size (KERC, 25 Aug 2026). Compare BESCOM's ₹5.80 per unit domestic rate.
- **31 March**: date Tamil Nadu net-metering credits lapse (TNERC).
- **Above 100 kW**: size above which Maharashtra's draft 2026 rules would require storage. This does not affect typical homes (draft, reported 23 Sep 2026).

## What is the difference between on-grid, hybrid and off-grid?

| | On-grid | Hybrid | Off-grid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Connected to DISCOM grid | Yes, with net or bidirectional meter | Yes | No |
| Battery | No | Yes | Yes, essential |
| Works during a power cut | No. The inverter shuts down for safety. | Yes, from the battery, for backed-up loads | Yes |
| Exports surplus to grid | Yes, credited under state rules | Depends on configuration and state approval | No |
| PM Surya Ghar subsidy | Yes | Yes, if approved by your SERC, on panel kW only | No |
| Relative cost | Lowest | Higher, because of the battery | Highest for the same reliability |

## Which systems qualify for the PM Surya Ghar subsidy?

MNRE's operational guidelines say:

- **Grid-connected systems** "tagged to a particular residential power connection" qualify. All metering arrangements the state regulator approves are covered, including net metering, gross metering, net billing, virtual and group net metering.
- **Systems that are grid-connected but do not export**, which MNRE calls "behind-the-meter systems, battery hybrid systems etc.", "shall be eligible for CFA under the scheme subject to approval by the respective Electricity Regulatory Commissions". The DISCOM inspects them and checks the reverse power relay protection.
- **Off-grid installations** "will not be eligible for CFA under the scheme."
- **Batteries and other add-ons are allowed**, but "the CFA calculation shall be based on ... capacity of solar modules installed". A battery does not raise your subsidy.
- **Inverter size does not matter** for the subsidy. It is paid on the rated DC capacity of the panels.

In practice, if you want a hybrid, ask your DISCOM and vendor before you sign whether your state allows the exact configuration for the subsidy. Make sure the application on the portal describes it correctly.

## How are exports credited on on-grid systems?

That depends on your state regulator, and it is the main thing that decides how much an on-grid system earns:

- **Karnataka** settles monthly. Surplus is bought at ₹1.96–₹2.58 per unit for subsidised home systems, or ₹3.89 without subsidy. Under net metering, fixed charges stay "regardless of availability of excess electricity".
- **Tamil Nadu** carries net-metered surplus units forward each month, but they **lapse on 31 March**. Net feed-in pays ₹3.61 per unit instead.
- **Kolkata (CESC)** pays ₹4.80 per unit for LV net billing, locked for the system's life.

See [Net metering in India by state](/in/guides/net-metering-by-state) for the details.

When export rates are low, using your own solar matters more. A battery is one way to do that. Shifting daytime loads such as washing machines, pumps and water heaters into sunny hours is cheaper.

## When does a hybrid (battery) system make sense?

A battery adds cost and does not add subsidy, so it needs a clear reason:

- **Frequent or long power cuts.** This is the strongest case. MNRE's technical specifications require every grid-tied rooftop system to have islanding protection, so the inverter turns off when the grid fails. Without a battery, you have no solar during an outage.
- **You already pay for an inverter-battery backup.** A hybrid can replace that setup and charge from solar.
- **Very low export rates with evening-heavy use.** The battery can store daytime surplus for the evening rather than exporting it cheaply. Whether that pays depends on battery cost and life, and we do not yet have a sourced Indian battery price to compute it.

A battery does **not** remove fixed charges. MPERC's FY 2026-27 order says rooftop solar consumers still rely on the grid "for backup supply, seasonal variations, and net metering adjustments" and must pay fixed charges.

## When does off-grid make sense?

Mostly where there is no reliable grid connection, or no connection at all. Off-grid gets no PM Surya Ghar subsidy. The system also has to be sized for the worst days of the monsoon, which means a much bigger battery. For a home with a normal DISCOM connection, on-grid or hybrid is almost always better value.

## Are batteries becoming mandatory?

Not for homes. Maharashtra's draft 2026 rooftop regulations, as reported by Energetica India on 23 September 2026, would require an energy storage system for **new grid-interactive systems above 100 kW**. Typical home systems are 1–10 kW. Karnataka's 2026 regulations say KERC will review the need for distributed storage later.

## Which should I choose?

- **Reliable grid, high bills:** on-grid. It is the cheapest per unit and fully subsidised. See [Is rooftop solar worth it in India?](/in/guides/is-rooftop-solar-worth-it-india)
- **Frequent outages:** hybrid. Confirm SERC approval for the subsidy first.
- **No grid:** off-grid, without PM Surya Ghar subsidy.

Before signing, compare quotes from vendors registered on the National Portal, and read [PM Surya Ghar subsidy 2026](/in/guides/pm-surya-ghar-subsidy-2026).

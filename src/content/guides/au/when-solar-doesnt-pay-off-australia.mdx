Rooftop solar pays back within about 4 to 6.5 years for a typical 6.6 kW system in Australia's capital cities, according to CHOICE's January 2026 figures. But those are averages. Solar disappoints when most of its output is exported at a feed-in tariff that can now be near zero, when midday grid power is already free under the Solar Sharer Offer, when the roof is small or shaded, or when you sell before the savings catch up with the cost.

## Key numbers

- **Payback for a 6.6 kW system (CHOICE/SolarQuotes defaults, January 2026):** **3 years 9 months** (Darwin) to **6 years 5 months** (Melbourne).
- **Victoria's feed-in floor:** **0 c/kWh**. No minimum feed-in tariff since **1 July 2025** (ESC).
- **Solar Sharer:** up to **24 kWh** free in a **3-hour** midday window for opted-in households in NSW, SE Qld and SA from **1 July 2026**; other hours cost **1-4 c/kWh** more (AER).
- **Battery payback (CHOICE, $8,500 installed, 7 kWh overnight use):** **6.3 years** (Adelaide) to **20.7 years** (Hobart); battery warranties are typically **10 years**.
- **Installed solar price:** about **$1,000 per kW** rule of thumb (SolarQuotes, October 2026).

## 1. You use little power while the sun is up

Solar saves the most on power you use yourself, because that replaces retail electricity. Power you export earns only the feed-in tariff. CHOICE puts it plainly: "With daytime FiTs dropping, it's best to use your own solar power as much as you can." If the house is empty all day and most use is in the evening, a large share of output gets exported at a low rate. Timers on the dishwasher, washing machine, pool pump and hot water help. If none of those apply, expect a slower payback than the averages.

## 2. Your feed-in tariff is close to zero

In Victoria, the [Essential Services Commission](https://www.esc.vic.gov.au/electricity-and-gas/electricity-and-gas-tariffs-and-benchmarks/minimum-feed-tariff) "no longer sets minimum feed-in tariffs". From 1 July 2025, retailers may set their own, but they "cannot be below zero ($0.00) cents per kWh." CHOICE adds that some new plans cap feed-in tariffs during the day or pay less once you pass a threshold.

If a quote's savings depend on exporting most of your output, ask what feed-in rate it assumed, and check your retailer's current rate. Our [feed-in tariffs by state](/au/guides/feed-in-tariff-comparison-by-state) page has the regulated rates and benchmarks.

## 3. Solar Sharer already makes midday power free

From 1 July 2026, households with a smart meter in NSW, south-east Queensland and South Australia can opt in to the [Solar Sharer Offer](/au/guides/solar-sharer-free-electricity-explained). The AER set 3 free hours each day (11 am to 2 pm, or 12 pm to 3 pm in SA), up to 24 kWh.

This cuts into solar's value. If you're on that offer, panels save nothing on the power you use in those 3 hours, because it's already free. Your solar still helps in the morning and afternoon, and exports still earn a feed-in tariff, but the best-paying part of a midday solar day is gone. Households considering solar mainly to cut midday use should price the SSO first. (This is our reading of how the tariff works; the AER says it is "best suited to customers who can shift their usage into the free usage period.")

## 4. Your roof is small, shaded or poorly oriented

Small systems cost more per kW. SolarQuotes' October 2026 ranges put a 3 kW system at $3,500 to $5,500, which is about $1,170 to $1,830 per kW, against roughly $900 to $1,200 per kW for 10 kW. Shade from trees or neighbouring buildings and a roof facing away from the sun cut output further. Ask any installer for a shade analysis and expected yearly output for your actual roof, not a generic figure.

## 5. You'll move before it pays back

A 4- to 6-year payback assumes you stay that long. If you sell sooner, you only recover the rest if buyers pay more for the panels, which nobody can promise. Batteries carry an extra condition: the [CER](https://cer.gov.au/batteries) says a battery claimed under the rebate should stay at the property for the warranty period or until the end of 2030, whichever is later, and moving it to another premises makes it ineligible.

## 6. You bought a battery to chase the rebate

The federal rebate made batteries cheaper, but CHOICE's 2026 battery examples still show long paybacks in some cities:

| City | Battery payback (CHOICE) |
|---|---|
| Adelaide | 6.3 years |
| Brisbane | 7.7 years |
| Sydney | 8.3 years |
| Canberra | 12.1 years |
| Melbourne | 13.9 years |
| Hobart | 20.7 years |

CHOICE notes battery warranties are typically 10 years. A battery with a payback longer than its warranty may never pay for itself. CHOICE also reports that under the earlier flat rebate, some households "super-sized" their batteries. Tiered rebates since 1 May 2026 make oversizing even less worthwhile. See [when a battery is not worth it](/au/guides/when-a-battery-is-not-worth-it).

## 7. The quote is too high, or too good to be true

At about $1,000 per kW as SolarQuotes' rule of thumb, a quote far above the ranges in our [cost by system size guide](/au/guides/solar-panel-cost-by-system-size) stretches the payback. Very high savings claims are the other red flag. Ask for the assumptions in writing: daytime use, feed-in rate, and expected output. Our [battery rebate scams guide](/au/guides/battery-rebate-scams) covers the pitches to watch for.

## When solar still pays

None of this means solar is a bad buy. It pays best when you're home in the day or can shift use, when you have an EV or electric hot water to soak up midday output, and when the system is sized to what you use. For the positive case, read [is solar still worth it with low feed-in tariffs](/au/blog/is-solar-still-worth-it-low-feed-in-tariffs) and [is a solar battery worth it in Australia](/au/guides/is-a-solar-battery-worth-it-australia).

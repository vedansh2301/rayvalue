Under Jamaica's net billing arrangement, the solar power you use at home saves you the full price of a JPS kilowatt-hour, and the power you send to the grid is bought by JPS at a separate, lower monthly rate. The Office of Utilities Regulation (OUR) validates that rate each month as JPS's "short-term variable avoided cost of generation plus a premium of fifteen percent". In 2025 it ranged from J$19.17 to J$29.23 per kWh, well below the roughly J$59 per kWh that JPS earned on average from residential customers.

## Key numbers

- **J$19.171 to J$29.233 per kWh**: the monthly net billing energy rate across 2025 (OUR notices, latest dated 2026 February 3).
- **J$22.22 per kWh**: simple average of the twelve 2025 monthly rates.
- **15%**: the renewable energy premium added to JPS's short-term avoided cost.
- **0 to 100 kW**: the size of facilities covered by the net billing rates.
- **US$0.37 per kWh**: JPS average revenue per kWh from residential customers in 2025, about J$59 at the J$159.64 average billing exchange rate in its annual report.
- **1,817 kWh**: average annual use per JPS residential customer in 2025.

## How is the net billing rate set?

The OUR notices state that the net billing arrangement is governed by the Electricity Act, 2015, the Electricity (Net Billing) Regulations, 2022, the OUR Act, the National Energy Policy, 2009, the 2016 Electricity Licence and the OUR's Standard Offer Contract determination. The rate "shall satisfy the principle of the Short-term variable Avoided Cost of generation plus a premium of fifteen percent (15%) allowed for Renewable Energy (RE) Generation".

The avoided cost "mainly reflects the cost of fuel used for electricity generation on a monthly basis". JPS calculates it by dividing the total fuel cost for the billing month by net system generation, then submits its figures to the OUR, which reviews and validates them. That is why the rate moves month to month with fuel prices.

## Net billing vs net metering: why it matters

With net metering, every kWh you export would cancel a kWh you buy at the retail price. Jamaica does not work that way. Exports earn the avoided-cost rate, so a kWh you use yourself is worth much more than a kWh you export:

| What happens to a solar kWh | Approximate value (2025) |
|---|---|
| Used in your home | Avoids buying from JPS: about J$59 per kWh on average for residential customers |
| Exported to the grid | Paid at the net billing rate: J$19 to J$29 per kWh |

The J$59 figure is an average across all residential revenue, including fixed charges, so your own marginal saving depends on your tariff block and fuel charge. The gap still holds: self-consumption is worth roughly two to three times an export.

## What this means for sizing a system

According to [PVGIS](https://re.jrc.ec.europa.eu/api/v5_3/PVcalc?lat=17.971&lon=-76.793&peakpower=1&loss=14&optimalangles=1&outputformat=json), 1 kWp of panels in Kingston produces about 1,658 kWh a year. The average JPS residential customer used 1,817 kWh in 2025. So for an average home, a system of a little over 1 kWp would match annual use on paper; anything much larger will mostly export at the lower rate.

- **Size for daytime use**: air conditioning, fridges, water pumps and home businesses soak up midday power.
- **Shift loads to the day**: run washers and pumps when the sun is out.
- **Consider a battery only for backup or high evening use**: storing a kWh to avoid buying at about J$59 can beat exporting it for about J$22, but battery costs need a sourced quote.

## How do you get on net billing?

The OUR notices refer to customers or self-generators supplying energy from small renewable facilities of up to 100 kW. You will need a JPS-approved interconnection and a bi-directional meter, and your installer should handle the application with JPS. We have not yet summarised the application steps from the 2022 regulations, so check the current requirements with JPS before you buy.

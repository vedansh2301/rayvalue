Solar can be worth it in Alberta, and the rules are friendlier than in most provinces. Calgary gets about as much solar output per panel as Toronto, and small micro-generators are credited for exports at their retail energy rate. The catch is that delivery charges stay on your bill whatever you generate, and the credit you get depends on the retailer contract you choose.

## Key numbers

- **1,252 kWh per kW per year** in Calgary and **1,161** in Edmonton (NREL PVWatts v8, run 22 Nov 2026). Toronto is 1,276 and Vancouver 1,019 on the same settings.
- **Under 150 kW**: the "small micro-generator" class that most homes fall into (Government of Alberta).
- **Retail rate**: small micro-generators are credited monthly for exports at their retail rates, or can opt for hourly wholesale prices with a suitable meter (Government of Alberta).
- **Once a year**: retailers must pay out unused credits annually (Utilities Consumer Advocate).
- **Delivery charges are not reduced** by generating your own power (Utilities Consumer Advocate).
- **Federal Greener Homes Grant: closed**. The last day to upload documents was 31 Dec 2025 (NRCan).

## Does Alberta get enough sun for solar?

Yes. On the same modelling settings (1 kW, south-facing, 20 degree tilt, 14% losses), NREL's PVWatts gives:

| City | Modelled output, kWh per kW per year |
|---|---|
| Toronto | 1,276 |
| Montréal | 1,267 |
| Calgary | 1,252 |
| Edmonton | 1,161 |
| Vancouver | 1,019 |

Calgary is within 2% of Toronto. Edmonton is about 7% lower than Calgary but still well above Vancouver. A 6 kW array in Calgary would make about 7,500 kWh a year (6 x 1,252). Snow cover in winter cuts output in some months, but most production comes from April to September anyway.

## How does Alberta pay you for solar exports?

Alberta does not use net metering in the Ontario sense. It uses the Micro-generation Regulation under the Electric Utilities Act. The Government of Alberta explains two classes:

- **Small micro-generators (under 150 kW)** are "credited for the electricity sent back to the grid on a monthly basis at their retail rates". They can also install a suitable meter to be credited at hourly wholesale prices instead.
- **Large micro-generators (150 kW and up)** are credited at the hourly wholesale price.

The Utilities Consumer Advocate (UCA) calls this net billing. Credits usually offset your monthly bill. If you do not use them all, "the retailer must financially compensate consumers for unused credits once a year".

This is a real advantage. In several provinces, exports earn less than imports. Under Alberta's rules for small systems, an exported kWh earns the same energy rate you pay to import one.

## What is the catch?

**Delivery charges.** The UCA is clear: "the cost of delivering energy is largely fixed, and consumers cannot reduce it by generating their own electricity." You will still pay distribution charges, transmission charges, local access fees and similar items. Solar reduces only the energy part of the bill.

**Your retailer sets the rate.** The Alberta government says: "The rate at which you are credited is agreed upon between you and your electricity retailer. The government does not decide what this rate should be." Some retailers offer plans built for solar owners. Read the terms: the credit rate, the import rate, how long the price is fixed and any exit fees all matter.

**You may need a new contract.** The UCA says you may need to amend your current contract, and advises confirming with your retailer how and when credits are paid before you buy panels.

## Is there a solar rebate in Alberta?

We could not confirm any province-wide solar rebate for Alberta homes in 2026. The federal Canada Greener Homes Grant, which used to help, is closed. Some municipalities have run solar financing programs. Check your city's website before you sign anything, and see [what's left after Canada Greener Homes closed](/ca/guides/canada-greener-homes-closed-whats-left).

## What does it cost to connect?

Less than you might expect. The Government of Alberta says individual micro-generators "do not have to pay for the ordinary and reasonable costs of interconnection and meter infrastructure", because those costs are shared across all customers in the distribution company's area.

You still need to apply. The steps are: find your distribution company on your bill, follow the Alberta Utilities Commission (AUC) micro-generation guidelines with an electrical contractor, submit the AUC notice and your application to the distribution company, and sign an interconnection agreement. The system must meet an emissions test of 418 kg per MWh or less, which solar easily passes.

## Who does solar suit in Alberta?

- **Homeowners in southern Alberta** with a south, east or west-facing roof and little shade. Calgary's modelled output matches Toronto's.
- **Homes with larger bills**, where the energy share of the bill is big enough to matter next to fixed delivery charges.
- **People willing to shop for a retailer plan.** Because exports are credited at your retail rate, the plan you pick affects both what you pay and what you earn.

## How do I check if it pays for my home?

1. **Pull a year of bills.** Separate the energy charges from delivery and fixed charges. Solar only reduces the energy part.
2. **Estimate output.** Use the PVWatts figures above as a guide: about 1,250 kWh per kW in Calgary and 1,160 in Edmonton.
3. **Value each kWh at your energy rate** under the retailer plan you would choose.
4. **Compare against two or three quotes.** Divide the installed cost by the yearly energy savings for a rough payback range. Use a low and a high energy rate, not one number.

If the payback is longer than you plan to stay in the home, or longer than the panel warranty, solar may not be worth it for you. For more on that, read [when solar doesn't pay in Canada](/ca/blog/when-solar-doesnt-pay-canada).

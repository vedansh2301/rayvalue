Solar does not pay everywhere in Canada. It struggles where electricity is cheap, where exports earn less than imports, where credits expire before you use them, and where most of the bill is fixed charges that panels cannot touch. If two or more of the situations below apply to your home, get very careful quotes or skip it.

## Key numbers

- **7.065¢ per kWh**: Hydro-Québec Rate D price for the first 40 kWh a day, from 1 April 2026 (Hydro-Québec rates).
- **4.730¢ per kWh**: what Hydro-Québec pays for unused net metering surplus when the bank resets every 24 months (same source).
- **10¢ vs 11.87¢ to 14.08¢**: BC Hydro's export price under RS 2289 against its Tier 1 and Tier 2 energy prices (BC Hydro, checked 22 Nov 2026).
- **12 months**: how long Ontario net metering credits can be carried forward (Hydro One).
- **1,019 kWh per kW per year** in Vancouver vs 1,276 in Toronto (NREL PVWatts v8, run 22 Nov 2026).
- **Federal Greener Homes Grant: closed** (NRCan).

## 1. Your electricity is very cheap

The value of solar is the power you avoid buying. In Quebec, Rate D charges 7.065¢ for the first 40 kWh a day and 11.142¢ after that (rates from 1 April 2026). At Montréal's modelled output of 1,267 kWh per kW, one kW of panels avoids about $90 a year at the lower price and $141 at the higher one.

A home that uses little power and stays mostly within the first 40 kWh a day will see values near the bottom of that range. Hydro-Québec's grant of $1,000 per kW helps a lot, but it is capped at 40% of cost, so most of the price is still yours. See [Subvention solaire Hydro-Québec](/ca/guides/subvention-solaire-hydro-quebec) for the details.

## 2. You will export more than you use

When exports are worth less than imports, oversized systems waste value.

- **BC**: new customers on RS 2289 get a fixed 10¢ per kWh for exports, against 11.87¢ (Tier 1) or 14.08¢ (Tier 2) for imports.
- **Quebec**: surplus kWh go into a bank. When it resets every 24 months, the balance is paid at 4.730¢, the average supply cost. That is less than half the 11.142¢ upper price.
- **Ontario with the rebate**: Home Renovation Savings systems are for "load displacement only" and cannot use net metering. Exports earn no credit.

Rule of thumb: size the system to the power you will use on site, not to the roof area. A battery can help in BC and Ontario, but it adds cost.

## 3. Your credits expire before winter ends

Ontario net metering credits can be carried forward "for a consecutive 12-month period", per Hydro One. A large summer surplus that you cannot use within 12 months is lost. In Quebec, the bank resets every 24 months and the leftover is paid at only 4.730¢.

If your plan depends on banking summer power for winter heating, check how your utility's credit window lines up with your bills.

## 4. Most of your bill is fixed charges

Solar reduces energy charges, not fixed ones. Alberta's Utilities Consumer Advocate says "the cost of delivering energy is largely fixed, and consumers cannot reduce it by generating their own electricity", so you still pay distribution, transmission and local access fees. Hydro One says fixed charges such as the monthly service charge cannot be offset by net metering credits. Hydro-Québec's system access charge of 46.154¢ a day stays on the bill too.

Look at last year's bills. If the energy line is a small part of the total, the savings will be small no matter how many panels you install.

## 5. You get less sun than the rest of Canada

On the same settings, NREL's PVWatts models 1,019 kWh per kW a year in Vancouver, about 20% less than Toronto's 1,276. Shade from trees or neighbouring buildings, or a north-facing roof, can cut output much further. Each lost kWh stretches the payback.

Ask installers for a shading analysis and a production estimate for your actual roof, not a regional average.

## 6. You don't qualify for the rebate you were counting on

Rebates have rules that exclude many homes:

- **Ontario**: you must own and live in a detached, semi-detached, row house, townhome or mobile home on a permanent foundation. Condo apartments are not listed. You must get written pre-approval before buying or installing anything.
- **Quebec**: panels must be new, certified to CSA 22.2 no 61730 and CAN/CSA-IEC 61215, bought in Quebec and installed from 30 June 2025, with the connection request done by a master electrician.
- **Federal**: the Greener Homes Grant is closed. 31 December 2025 was the last day to upload documents.

A quote that assumes a rebate you will not get is a quote that is wrong by thousands of dollars.

## 7. You will move before it pays back

Payback in Canada usually takes years. If you expect to sell before the system has paid for itself, you rely on the next buyer valuing the panels, which is uncertain. The same applies if your roof needs replacing soon: removing and reinstalling panels adds cost. Re-roof first if the roof is near the end of its life.

## How do I spot an overstated quote?

- **It promises a federal grant.** The Greener Homes Grant is closed.
- **It values every kWh at your highest price.** Exports in BC earn 10¢; Quebec surplus can end up at 4.730¢; Ontario rebate systems get nothing for exports.
- **It ignores fixed charges.** The savings line should only reduce energy charges.
- **It uses one payback number.** Ask for a low and a high case.
- **It comes from a cold call.** NRCan and the Ontario program both warn about unsolicited contact and fake rebate offers.

## Is solar ever worth it in these cases?

Sometimes. A household that uses most of its output on site, has a good unshaded roof, qualifies for a provincial rebate and plans to stay can still come out ahead. See [is solar worth it in Canada in 2026](/ca/blog/is-solar-worth-it-canada-2026) for where the numbers are strongest, and [Ontario solar rebate vs net metering](/ca/guides/ontario-solar-rebate-vs-net-metering) for one of the most common decisions.

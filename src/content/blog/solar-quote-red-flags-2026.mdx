The biggest red flag in a 2026 solar quote is any mention of a federal tax credit for a home system. The IRS says the residential credit is not available for property placed in service after December 31, 2025. Other warning signs: pressure to sign today, a loan price far above the cash price, savings based on steep rate increases, and production or export assumptions that do not match your utility's rules.

## Key numbers

- **December 31, 2025:** the last date property could be placed in service for the 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit (IRS).
- **$2.4 to $6.3 per watt:** range of median prices across the top-100 U.S. residential installers in 2025. About 60% were below $4 and about 10% above $5 (Berkeley Lab, August 2026).
- **$30:** FPL's minimum base bill for net metering customers (FPL).
- **About $24 a month:** SDG&E's Base Services Charge, which solar credits cannot offset (SDG&E).
- **18.16 cents per kWh:** U.S. average residential price in August 2026, up from 17.57 cents a year earlier; Florida's fell from 15.33 to 14.99 cents (EIA).

## The 10 red flags

| # | Red flag | What to ask or do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "You'll get 30% back from the federal tax credit" | Ask for the IRS source. The IRS says it is not available after December 31, 2025 |
| 2 | Pressure to decide today, or a demand to pay in cash | The FTC says to avoid companies that pressure you for a quick decision |
| 3 | Only a monthly payment, no cash price | Ask for the cash price and price per watt |
| 4 | Loan price much higher than the cash price | Ask for the dealer fee in writing |
| 5 | Savings assume big yearly rate increases | Ask what rate increase is used and why |
| 6 | System sized to export lots of power in California | Check against your utility's export values |
| 7 | Bill shown going to $0 | Fixed charges remain: $30 FPL minimum, about $24 SDG&E and PG&E Base Services Charge |
| 8 | Lease or PPA with an escalator you did not notice | The FTC says to check whether, when and by how much payments rise |
| 9 | No license or a vague company history | The FTC says to check licenses and complaints |
| 10 | California property-tax promise for a 2027 install | The section 73 exclusion ends for new systems on January 1, 2027 |

## 1. A federal tax credit that no longer exists

The IRS page for the Residential Clean Energy Credit says it covered 30% of costs for property installed from 2022 through December 31, 2025, and "is not available for any property placed in service after December 31, 2025." A 2026 quote that subtracts 30% for this credit is wrong. If you are offered a lease or PPA, read [solar lease vs buy](/guides/solar-lease-vs-buy) for how tax credits work when a company owns the system, and get any price promise in the contract.

## 2. Pressure tactics

The FTC's advice is to compare detailed bids from several companies and to be wary of any company that "pressures you for a quick decision." Expiring "today only" discounts, or a salesperson who will not leave without a signature, are reasons to slow down.

## 3 and 4. Hidden financing costs

Berkeley Lab found that loan-financed residential systems in 2025 were "considerably higher priced than cash-purchase systems," possibly because loan origination fees were rolled into the upfront price. Ask for the cash price and the loan price side by side, and the APR and fees. See [how to compare solar quotes](/guides/how-to-compare-solar-quotes).

## 5. Rate increase assumptions

Rates move both ways. EIA's August figures show the U.S. average up from 17.57 to 18.16 cents per kWh over the year, while Florida fell from 15.33 to 14.99 cents and California from 30.92 to 29.75 cents. The FTC notes "it's hard to predict future utility rates." A quote that only works with large yearly increases is fragile. Our calculators show low, likely and high ranges.

## 6. Ignoring California export rules

Under NEM 3.0, PG&E's 2026 export credit on an April weekday at 1 p.m. is about 0.4 cents per kWh. A quote that values every exported kWh at the retail price overstates savings. See [California NEM 3.0 export rates by utility](/guides/california-solar-export-rates-by-utility).

## 7. "Your bill will be zero"

FPL keeps a $30 minimum base bill for net metering customers. In California, SDG&E's Base Services Charge "is not eligible to be offset by applied generation credits," and PG&E charges a similar amount. See [California's Base Services Charge](/guides/california-base-services-charge-solar).

## 8. Lease and PPA fine print

The FTC suggests asking whether payments will increase, when and by how much, and whether you can transfer the contract to a buyer if you sell. Get the answers in the contract.

## 9. Licensing and track record

The FTC says to ask whether the company has the licenses, certificates or bonding your state, county and city require, and to check with consumer protection agencies and the contractor licensing board.

## 10. California property-tax claims

The Board of Equalization says the section 73 exclusion becomes inoperative on January 1, 2027. A promise that a 2027 install will be "property-tax free" needs a source. See [the property-tax deadline guide](/guides/california-solar-property-tax-exclusion-ends-2027).

Most UK solar installers are honest, but the boom in demand brings pressure sellers with it. The biggest red flags are an installer that is not MCS-certified, VAT added to a home install before April 2027, talk of "free government solar panels", export rates that only apply to someone else's customers, and a price far above the official cost data. Each of these is easy to check before you sign.

## Key numbers

- **MCS certification**: needed for SEG export payments from suppliers such as E.ON Next and Utilita, and for the Warm Homes Loan.
- **0% VAT** on installing solar and batteries in homes until 31 March 2027 (HMRC Notice 708/6).
- **£1,546 to £1,595 per kW**: median installed cost of MCS solar systems in 2025/26, by size band, excluding batteries (DESNZ).
- **31 December 2026**: ECO4 ends (Ofgem).
- **£36,000**: usual household income limit for the Warm Homes: Local Grant in England (GOV.UK).

## Is the installer MCS-certified?

This is the first check, because so much depends on it. MCS is the certification scheme for small renewable installations, and it has a public installer search. Without an MCS certificate (or an equivalent), you may not be able to get paid for exports:

- E.ON Next asks for an installation that has been "Microgeneration Certification Scheme (MCS) certified or has Flexi-Orb certification".
- Utilita says installations must meet recognised certification standards "such as MCS or equivalent".
- The Warm Homes Loan Scheme works with MCS-certified installers.

Look the company up on the MCS site yourself rather than trusting a logo on a leaflet. Many installers are also members of RECC, the Renewable Energy Consumer Code, which keeps a searchable member directory.

## Are they adding VAT?

Until 31 March 2027, installing solar panels and batteries in a home is zero-rated, according to HMRC. A quote for a domestic install that adds 20% VAT should prompt a question. From 1 April 2027 the rate becomes 5%, not 20%.

Also be wary of the opposite: "Sign today, VAT is going up next week." The deadline is 31 March 2027. See [the VAT deadline guide](/uk/guides/solar-panels-vat-deadline-2027).

## Are they offering "free government solar panels"?

There is no universal UK solar grant. What does exist is narrow:

| Scheme | Who it is for | Status |
|---|---|---|
| ECO4 | Lower-income and vulnerable households, via energy suppliers | Runs to 31 Dec 2026 (Ofgem) |
| Warm Homes: Local Grant | England, privately owned homes with EPC D–G, household income usually £36,000 or less | Open, through councils (GOV.UK) |
| Warm Homes Loan | UK-wide; loans up to £15,000 for solar and £15,000 for a battery | A loan, not a grant (GOV.UK) |
| Home Energy Scotland | Scotland | No grant or loan for standard solar PV (HES) |

Someone who cold-calls promising free panels "from the government" with no checks on income or EPC should be treated with caution. A loan is not a grant, however it is described.

## Is the export rate real, and will you get it?

Some sales pitches quote the highest export rate on the market. The 25p rate from Good Energy, for example, is only for customers who had their solar and battery installed by Good Energy, for 12 months, before rolling onto its standard 12p tariff. Open-to-anyone rates are much lower: Octopus SEG pays 4.1p, for example. Ask the installer which tariff they used in the savings estimate and check you qualify. Our [SEG comparison](/uk/guides/best-seg-rates) lists who can get each rate.

## Is the price far above the official data?

DESNZ publishes installed costs from the MCS database. In 2025/26 the median was £1,595 per kW for systems up to 4 kW and £1,546 per kW for 4–10 kW, excluding batteries. Divide the panel part of your quote by the system size in kW and compare. Something well above the median needs a reason, such as difficult access or extra electrical work.

## Do the savings figures add up?

Ask for the expected yearly generation in kWh, the share they assume you use at home, and the unit and export rates used. Then check:

- **Generation.** PVGIS, the European Commission's solar model, gives about 1,023 kWh per kWp a year for an optimally tilted system in London, and less further north. A quote that claims much more per kWp than PVGIS for your area is optimistic.
- **Self-use.** An estimate that assumes you use nearly all of it at home, with nobody in during the day and no battery, is optimistic.
- **Price rises.** Some estimates assume electricity prices rise every year. Ask to see the figures with flat prices too.

## Other warning signs

- Pressure to sign on the day, or a "discount" that expires tonight.
- A large deposit before any survey.
- No written quote with panel, inverter and battery models, warranties and the VAT line.
- Finance arranged by the salesperson without showing you the interest rate and total cost.

## FAQ

**How many quotes should I get?** At least three, all from MCS-certified installers, priced for the same system size.

**Can I cancel?** Check the contract's cancellation terms before you sign, and ask the installer to point them out. RECC members also follow its consumer code.

**Is a battery always worth adding?** No. See [Home batteries and time-of-use tariffs](/uk/guides/solar-battery-worth-it-uk) and [When solar panels don't pay](/uk/blog/when-solar-panels-dont-pay-uk).

Owned solar panels rarely stop a house sale, as long as you can show who installed them, that they were certified and that building regulations were met. The real risk is leased "free" panels, where a company rents your roof for decades. UK Finance and the Building Societies Association say most lenders' mortgage conditions require their consent to such an arrangement, and a buyer's lender may refuse to lend on a property that has one.

## Key numbers

- **Most lenders** require consent before you grant a lease (or, in Northern Ireland, a "Lease of Rights") of your roof for solar panels (UK Finance/BSA, checked 22 Nov 2026).
- **2013**: date of the UK Finance/BSA minimum requirements for roof-space leases in England and Wales (version 2, 18 March 2013), referred to in the NI guidance.
- **MCS certificate**: one of the records the government tells Warm Homes Loan borrowers to keep, alongside the quote, finance agreement, invoice and proof of purchase (DESNZ factsheet, 27 Oct 2026).
- **No official UK figure** exists, as far as we could find, for how much solar adds to a house price.

## Do solar panels cause problems when selling a house?

It depends on who owns them.

| | Panels you own | Panels on a roof lease |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the panels | You, then the buyer | The provider, for the lease term |
| Lender consent needed? | Not for the panels themselves | Usually yes, under most mortgage conditions |
| Risk to the sale | Low if paperwork is complete | Buyer's lender may refuse or ask for changes |
| Who earns the subsidy or export income | You | In the NI model UK Finance describes, the provider earns the subsidy and you get free electricity |

If you own the panels, list them clearly in the sale paperwork so the buyer knows they are included. Leased panels belong to someone else, who has rights over your roof for the length of the lease.

## Why do lenders worry about leased panels?

The UK Finance/BSA guidance for Northern Ireland explains the background. Providers offered "free installation of the panels onto roofs", earning money from renewables subsidies, while the homeowner got free electricity from the panels. That means a third party holds a long-term legal interest over part of the house.

For England and Wales, the guidance says that lenders' minimum requirements include evidence that the lease has been validly "contracted out" of the business tenancy protections of the Landlord and Tenant Act 1954. Northern Ireland law does not allow that, so there a "Lease of Rights" should be used instead of a lease.

On sales and remortgages, the guidance is direct. Where a borrower wants to buy or remortgage a property with an existing panel lease, the lender will review it against its requirements and "may request that the Lease of Rights is varied, or may choose not to lend on the property." It adds that "it is possible that the lender will refuse to lend against the property."

The guidance also warns that going ahead without consent, where it is required, "may be a breach of the mortgage terms and conditions".

## What should I do if I have leased panels?

1. **Find the lease** and any consent letter from your lender at the time.
2. **Check the provider still exists** and who to contact. Buyers' solicitors will ask.
3. **Ask the provider** whether the lease meets the UK Finance/BSA minimum requirements and whether they will help with variations or a buy-out.
4. **Tell your estate agent and solicitor early.** Lease problems found late can delay or end a sale.

## What paperwork do I need for owned panels?

Keep everything from the install:

- **MCS certificate.** The government's Warm Homes Loan factsheet lists it among the records to keep. It also shows the system was installed by a certified installer, which matters for export tariffs: see [SEG rates compared](/uk/guides/best-seg-rates).
- **Building regulations sign-off.** The Planning Portal says building regulations "will normally apply" to roof panels, covering the roof's ability to take the weight and the electrical work. An installer in a Competent Person Scheme can self-certify.
- **Planning position.** Most roof panels in England are permitted development, but not on listed buildings or some walls in conservation areas. See [solar panels and planning permission](/uk/guides/solar-panels-planning-permission).
- **Invoice, warranties and the inverter manual**, plus the export tariff contract, so the buyer can switch it into their name.
- **Any finance agreement.** If you bought the panels with a loan, check with the lender what happens to it when you sell.

## Do solar panels add value to a house?

We could not find official UK data that measures it. Claims of a fixed percentage uplift should be treated with caution unless they cite a study you can read. What a buyer gets is clearer: lower electricity bills, which depend on the roof, the system size and their own usage. Our [UK solar guide](/uk/guides/are-solar-panels-worth-it-uk) shows how to estimate them.

If you are installing now and might move in a few years, that matters more than resale value. Solar takes years to pay back, so read [when solar panels don't pay in the UK](/uk/blog/when-solar-panels-dont-pay-uk) before you commit.

## Should I accept "free" solar panels now?

Read the lease, ask your mortgage lender before signing, and think about the next owner. If you want panels without paying up front, owning them with a loan avoids the lease problem entirely; see the [Warm Homes Loan guide](/uk/guides/warm-homes-loan-solar-battery).

Most South African solar installers do honest work. The costly mistakes tend to follow a few patterns: no certificate of compliance, no registration with Eskom or the municipality, a "tax rebate" that ended in 2024, scare tactics about load-shedding, and savings promises built on export credits you won't get. Here are eight red flags and the rules that back each one.

## Key numbers and rules

- **Only a registered person** may issue a certificate of compliance (COC), after inspecting and testing (Electrical Installation Regulations, 2009, regulation 9).
- **No connection without a COC**: nobody may connect a completed or partly completed installation to the supply until a registered person has tested it and issued a COC (regulation 8(2)).
- **Electrical contractors must register annually** and employ a full-time registered person, or be one (regulation 6).
- **SSEG systems under 100 kW** connected to the network must be registered with Eskom or the municipality (Eskom, 26 March 2026).
- **Section 6C solar rebate:** available only for panels between **1 March 2023 and 29 February 2024** ([SARS](https://www.sars.gov.za/faq/faq-what-is-the-sars-solar-tax-benefit/)).
- **No load-shedding since 16 May 2025**, 504 days as of early October 2026 ([SAnews](https://www.sanews.gov.za/node/84735)).

## 1. "You don't need a COC for solar"

You do. Under the [Electrical Installation Regulations, 2009](https://www.nioh.ac.za/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/eir2009.pdf), every user or lessor of an electrical installation must have a valid COC with a test report (regulation 7(1)). After any addition or alteration, you need a COC for at least that work (regulation 7(4)). An inverter, battery and panels wired into your board are an addition. Only a registered person may issue the COC, and only after inspection and testing (regulation 9). Without one, you may struggle with insurance claims, registration and selling the house.

**Ask for:** the COC and test report in your name, the registered person's name and registration number, and the contractor's registration.

## 2. The installer isn't a registered electrical contractor

Regulation 6 says nobody may do electrical installation work as a contractor unless registered. Contractors must register every year, have a fixed address and telephone, and employ a registered person full-time or be one. A "solar company" that only supplies panels and sends a subcontractor with no papers is a warning sign.

## 3. "No need to register with Eskom or the City"

Eskom says national regulations require all SSEG systems under 100 kW that are connected to the network, including rooftop solar, to be registered with your supplier: Eskom or your municipality. It also explains that a COC confirms electrical safety, while registration ensures the system connects without harming the grid. You need both. Eskom waived registration charges for systems up to 50 kW until 30 September 2026.

The consequences are real. eThekwini's 2026/27 tariff booklet says unregistered generation is treated as unauthorised and unsafe, attracts penalties, and any unapproved credits billed may be reversed. In Cape Town, installers must apply to the City to authorise the system before it is installed, using a City-approved inverter.

## 4. "Claim the solar tax rebate"

The Section 6C rebate gave individuals 25% of the cost of new panels, up to R15,000. It applied only to panels brought into use between 1 March 2023 and 29 February 2024 (SARS). It is no longer available. A quote that deducts a solar tax rebate in 2026 is either out of date or misleading.

## 5. Load-shedding scare tactics

Eskom reported 504 consecutive days without load-shedding since 16 May 2025 (SAnews, October 2026). Outages can still happen, and backup has value. But a pitch built on "load-shedding is coming back next month", pushing you into the biggest battery, deserves a second quote. Today the case for solar rests mainly on tariffs. See [is solar worth it in South Africa without load-shedding](/za/blog/is-solar-worth-it-south-africa-without-load-shedding).

## 6. Savings that assume you sell lots of power back

Exports earn far less than imports:

- **Cape Town:** 107.98 c/kWh plus a 25 c incentive, before VAT, against a Home User rate of 309.52 c before VAT up to 600 kWh (City of Cape Town, 2026/27).
- **eThekwini:** 160.52 c/kWh before VAT, plus a Network Access Charge of R76.83 per kVA of inverter a month. Credits are not paid as cash (eThekwini, 2026/27).
- **Eskom Homeflex:** Gen-offset credits only up to what you consume in each time-of-use period (Eskom booklet 2026/27).

A spreadsheet that values every kWh at the full retail price, or promises monthly cash from exports in Durban, is wrong. See [feed-in tariffs by metro](/za/guides/solar-feed-in-tariffs-cape-town-johannesburg-durban).

## 7. An oversized inverter "for the future"

In eThekwini, the monthly Network Access Charge is based on inverter size in kVA. A 5 kVA inverter costs R384.15 a month before VAT whatever you export (5 x R76.83). Upsizing the inverter beyond your needs raises a fixed cost every month. On Eskom, larger supply sizes carry higher daily network charges. Ask why each component is the size it is.

## 8. Pressure to sign today, or large deposits

We have no source for typical South African deposit terms, so we won't quote a percentage. Common sense applies: get at least two quotes, pay deposits only to a registered company with a physical address (regulation 6 requires contractors to have one), and tie the final payment to receiving the COC and proof of registration with your supplier.

## A short checklist before you pay

1. Contractor registration and the registered person's details.
2. A written quote listing panels (kW), inverter (kVA), battery (usable kWh), brands and warranties.
3. Confirmation that the inverter is approved by your municipality or Eskom where required.
4. Registration with Eskom or the municipality before the system is connected.
5. COC and test report in your name at handover.
6. Savings based on your own bills and real export credits.
